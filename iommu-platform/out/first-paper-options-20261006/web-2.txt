increasingly integrate heterogeneous computing platforms, combining general-purpose processors with specialized accelerators such as AI engines, GPUs, and high-speed networking interfaces. This heterogeneity introduces challenges, as these accelerators and DMA-capable devices act as independent bus masters, directly accessing memory.\nL101: Consequently, ensuring both security and timing predictability in such environments becomes critical. To address these concerns, the Input-Output Memory Management Unit (IOMMU) plays a key role in mediating and regulating memory access, preventing unauthorized transactions while enforcing isolation and access control policies. While prior work has explored IOMMU-related side-channel vulnerabilities from a security standpoint, its role in performance interference remains largely unexplored.\nL102: Moreover, many of the same architectural properties that enable side-channel leakage, such as shared TLBs, caching effects, and translation overheads, can also introduce timing unpredictability. In this work, we analyze the contention effects within IOMMU structures using the Xilinx UltraScale+ ZCU104 platform, demonstrating how their shared nature introduce unpredictable delays.\nL103: Our findings reveal that IOMMU-induced interference primarily affects small memory transactions, where translation overheads significantly impact execution time. Additionally, we hypothesize that contention effects arising from IOTLBs exhibit similar behavior across architectures due to shared caching principles, such as prefetching and hierarchical TLB structures.\nL104: Notably, our experiments show that IOMMU interference can delay DMA transactions by up to 1.79× for lower-size transfers on the Arm SMMUv2 implementation.\nL105: ###### Index Terms:\nL106: \nL107: IOMMU, Interference, Contention, Mixed-Criticality Systems, Virtualization\nL108: ## I Introduction\nL109: In recent decades, the trend toward digitization [cite23†1 , cite24†2 ] has reshaped numerous industries, including automotive, robotics, and aerospace. This evolution has rapidly increased in system complexity, with high-end embedded platforms evolving from simple, single-core cite25†MCUs (cite25†MCUs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) into complex, heterogeneous architectures [cite26†3 ].\nL110: These modern systems integrate multi-core CPUs and specialized hardware accelerators such as cite27†GPUs (cite27†GPUs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ), cite28†TPUs (cite28†TPUs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ), cite29†NPUs (cite29†NPUs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ), and cite30†FPGA [cite31†4 , cite32†5 ], enabling unprecedented levels of performance and functionality.\nL111: At the same time, the demand for integration and efficiency has driven the consolidation of multiple workloads onto a single hardware platform, as integrating peripherals and computational tasks helps meet stringent cite33†SWaP-C (cite33†SWaP-C (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) requirements. This shift gave rise to Mixed Criticality Systems (MCSs), where systems with different criticality levels co-exist on the same hardware [cite34†6 ].\nL112: Consolidating mixed-criticality workloads onto a shared platform presents significant challenges in ensuring safety, security, and isolation [cite35†7 , cite26†3 ]. To address these challenges, virtualization has emerged as a key technology.\nL113: cite36†SPH (cite36†SPH (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) [cite37†8 , cite38†9 , cite39†10 , cite40†11 ] represent the zeitgeist of cite22†MCSs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) hypervisors, as they leverage static resource allocation to ensure isolation and determinism [cite41†12 , cite42†13 ].\nL114: While cite36†SPH (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) s effectively enforce spatial isolation by partitioning architectural resources (e.g.\nL115: memory regions and devices) among cite43†VMs (cite43†VMs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ), they cannot fully guarantee temporal isolation, as microarchitectural resources, including the cite44†LLC (cite44†LLC (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ), main memory, and system bus, remain inherently shared.\nL116: The resulting resource contention introduces two key challenges: (i) increased execution time and (ii) reduced determinism, caused by unpredictable delays [cite45†14 , cite46†15 , cite47†16 , cite48†17 , cite49†18 ]. These timing variations pose a significant challenge in hard real-time systems, where strict cite50†WCET (cite50†WCET (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) guarantees are essential to ensuring system correctness and safety [cite51†19 , cite48†17 ].\nL117: Over the past decade, considerable research has been conducted to identify and mitigate interference caused by shared micro-architectural resources.\nL118: The majority of this work has focused on three primary sources of contention: (i) the cite44†LLC (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) [cite52†20 , cite53†21 , cite54†22 ], (ii) the system bus [cite55†23 , cite56†24 , cite57†25 , cite58†26 ], and (iii) the main memory [cite55†23 , cite59†27 , cite60†28 , cite61†29 , cite62†30 , cite63†31 , cite63†31 ] - forming what we refer to as the ’Bermuda Triangle of Contention’.\nL119: While these sources have been well studied, modern platforms incorporate a broader array of shared resources (e.g., interrupt controllers [cite26†3 ]), whose effects on contention remain insufficiently explored.\nL120: Fig. 1: Conceptual overview of address translation for CPU and non-CPU bus-master devices, comparing MMU and IOMMU operations.\nL121: Among these overlooked components, the cite64†IOMMU (cite64†IOMMU (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) plays a critical role in enforcing memory isolation for non-CPU bus masters, preventing unauthorized memory access and enhancing system security [cite65†32 , cite66†33 ].\nL122: While IOMMUs are primarily recognized for these security functions, existing research has focused on a narrow set of concerns, mainly their susceptibility to side-channel attacks such as cite67†IOTLB (cite67†IOTLB (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) )-based timing attacks [cite65†32 , cite68†34 ].\nL123: In contrast, the implications of IOMMU design and usage on timing predictability in consolidated cite22†MCSs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) remain largely unexplored. In such systems, where multiple devices may simultaneously share IOMMU resources, contention in translation structures such as address translation overheads and IOTLB cache pressure can lead to unpredictable latency, undermining the determinism required for real-time applications.\nL124: This oversight is concerning, as such contention not only impacts system behavior but can also undermine timing determinism in real-time workloads.\nL125: Despite the critical role of the cite64†IOMMU (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) in cite22†MCSs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) , there has been little investigation into how contention for its internal resources may affect execution latency and predictability.\nL126: In this paper, we aim to advance the understanding of these effects through an empirical study of contention in a representative cite69†COTS (cite69†COTS (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) platform: the Xilinx UltraScale+ ZCU104. This platform integrates an cite64†IOMMU (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) compliant with the ARM SMMU-500 (SMMUv2) specification.\nL127: a decentralized architecture in which multiple Translation Buffer Units (TBUs) handle address translation for device clusters. We adapt established microbenchmarking techniques to the IOMMU domain to quantify the impact of shared translation structures under concurrent DMA traffic. Our contributions are as follows:\nL128:   1. 1.\nL129: Reverse-Engineering IOMMU Structures for Contention Analysis – In this work, we reverse-engineer the translation structures of the IOMMU implementation found in the ZCU104 platform. By uncovering the microarchitectural details of the IOMMU, we gain valuable insights into how translation caches, like the IOTLB, contribute to contention and DMA performance degradation.\nL130: This reverse-engineering effort enhances our understanding of IOMMU internals and provides procedures that can be applied in future research to analyze IOMMU contention across other platforms and architectures.\nL131:   2. 2.\nL132: \nL133: Characterizing Contention Effects on Translation Latency – Our experimental results show that IOMMU contention can introduce measurable latency increases (up to 1.79×) in DMA transactions. While moderate in magnitude, these delays may affect fine-grained timing behavior in real-time systems. We discuss these effects from a microarchitectural perspective and defer broader system-level implications to future work.\nL134: \nL135:   3. 3.\nL136: Open-Source Artifacts for Independent Validation and Further Exploration – To foster further research and validation, we make all experimental artifacts, including code, configurations, and data, publicly available to the research community. enabling independent validation and serving as a foundation for further research on IOMMU behavior across other hardware and system architectures.\nL137: ## II Background\nL138: Efficient cite70†DMA (cite70†DMA (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) has become essential in modern computing, allowing high-speed data transfers and improving overall system performance. Devices with DMA capabilities, such as hardware accelerators, rely on direct access to system memory, improving data processing speed and operational efficiency [cite71†35 ].\nL139: However, this direct access raises concerns about security and data integrity, particularly in consolidated computing environments where multiple systems share the same hardware. Ensuring both workload-specific isolation and system-wide protection requires robust mechanisms to control and restrict memory access. IOMMUs are pivotal to protect against such threats.\nL140: As shown on the left side of Figure cite72†1 , when a CPU issues a memory request, it uses a Virtual Address (VA) , which the MMU translates to a Physical Address (PA) by consulting its page tables, typically via a cite73†PTW (cite73†PTW (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ). This PA is then used to access the appropriate location in main memory .\nL141: Similarly, on the right side, when a non-CPU bus master (e.g., a DMA-capable device) initiates a memory transaction, it uses a virtual address within its assigned I/O address space. This address is intercepted by the IOMMU, which performs the translation to a physical address , accessing the I/O page tables via a cite73†PTW (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) to enforce isolation and access control policies.\nL142: The resulting PA is used to access the corresponding memory location . To perform these translations efficiently, the IOMMU maintains I/O page tables, analogous to those used by the MMU. Additionally, it uses cite67†IOTLB (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) s to cache recently translated address mappings and reduce translation latency.\nL143: The right side of Figure cite72†1 highlights two architectural models: (i) a centralized design, where a single shared IOTLB serves all devices; and (ii) a decentralized approach, where multiple IOTLBs are distributed across device clusters. These designs offer trade-offs in latency, scalability, and isolation granularity. By enforcing strict access control and isolating memory domains across devices, the IOMMU significantly enhances system reliability and security.\nL144: While the security benefits of cite64†IOMMU (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) s are well-documented, their impact on performance remains an underexplored area, particularly in cite22†MCSs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) . The overhead introduced by address translation, permission enforcement, and IOTLB management can create latency and contention when multiple devices share the same IOMMU.\nL145: This contention is especially concerning for real-time and safety-critical applications, where predictable timing is crucial. To provide context for our study, we briefly review the ARM cite74†SMMU (cite74†SMMU (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) ) architecture, which serves as the reference IOMMU implementation in our evaluation.\nL146: While other specifications exist, including Intel VT-d [cite75†36 ], AMD-Vi [cite76†37 ], and the RISC-V IOMMU [cite77†38 ], this work focuses specifically on ARM’s specification. The ARM SMMUv2 [cite78†39 ] is the standard IOMMU implementation for arm64 systems, providing secure memory isolation and efficient address translation. It employs a stream-based model, using StreamIDs and optional SubstreamIDs for fine-grained device identification.\nL147: SMMUv2 supports both stage-1 and stage-2 address translations and integrates tightly with ARM’s memory architecture. To improve lookup efficiency, it integrates IOTLBs at the interface between devices and the SMMU, reducing memory access overhead. Additionally, it defines three circular buffer queues for translation updates, fault handling, and page requests.\nL148: IOTLBs play a crucial role in accelerating address translation and reducing memory overhead, but their organization varies across IOMMU implementations. Centralized designs, such as those in Intel VT-d and AMD-Vi, simplify caching but can become bottlenecks under high traffic. In contrast, architectures like Arm’s CoreLink MMU-500 and MMU-600, as well as the RISC-V IOMMU open-source implementation from Zero-Day Labs [cite79†40 ], support more flexible or fully distributed IOTLB configurations.\nL149: While decentralized designs improve scalability and reduce lookup latency, they also introduce challenges in maintaining coherence and avoiding contention. These issues are not limited to specialized systems but extend to a wide range of cite69†COTS (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) platforms.\nL150: With growing adoption of distributed IOMMUs (e.g., Arm forecasts over 330 million MMU-600-based systems [cite80†41 ]), contention due to frequent invalidations, synchronization overhead, and I/O-intensive workloads becomes a critical concern.\nL151: This is particularly relevant in cite22†MCSs (Beyond the Bermuda Triangle of Contention: IOMMU Interference in Mixed Criticality Systems) , where timing predictability is essential, and in virtualized environments where devices assigned to different VMs compete for translation resources. Although this work focuses on a specific platform, our findings offer broader insight into the performance and scalability trade-offs posed by modern IOMMU designs.\nL152: ## III Methodology and Experimental Setup\nL153: \nL154: This section presents our methodology for evaluating IOMMU contention.
